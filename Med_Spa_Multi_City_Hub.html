The 7-question map of the industry

Med spa research collapses into seven questions. Each has its own tab above with sourced detail — this page is the summary.

The 7 questions

  1. Who can legally do what? Physicians can inject anywhere; NP/PA/RN authority is state-specific and often requires a supervising or collaborating physician; 36 states have zero med-spa-specific law.
  2. How is a med spa actually operated? Medical director (often part-time/remote) + 1–2 licensed injectors + estheticians + front desk, running on booking/EHR/CRM software with a consult→treat→rebook loop.
  3. What software stack do they use? Aesthetic-specific EMRs (Aesthetic Record, PatientNow, Symplast) for clinical/consent/lot-tracking; separate or bundled CRM/booking (Boulevard, Vagaro, Pabau, Zenoti).
  4. What procedures make the most money? Neurotoxins and GLP-1 programs have the best margins; fillers and lasers generate the most revenue per hour once volume scales.
  5. What's the unit economics? Median location revenue ~$1.86M/yr; net margins 15–25%; CAC ~$285; LTV:CAC of 3–4:1+ is healthy.
  6. What's happening with peptides / GLP-1? Real FDA approvals are narrow (semaglutide, tirzepatide, liraglutide); most "trending" peptides (BPC-157, retatrutide, exosomes) are unapproved, compounded-grey-area, or actively subject to FDA warning letters.
  7. Can a med spa become a subscription business? Yes, partially — memberships already produce 20–30% of revenue at leading practices and materially lift retention and LTV.

How a med spa actually runs

Regardless of state, most med spas share the same organizational shape and patient flow.

Typical staffing (single location)

Clinical leadership

Medical Director — MD/DO, legally responsible for medical decisions; frequently part-time or remote oversight rather than daily on-site presence. Owner may or may not be a clinician.

Injectors

1–2 licensed injectors (RN, NP, or PA) at early-stage practices; NPs often perform the highest volume and most complex injectable cases.

Support staff

1–2 estheticians (facials/peels/laser), a front-desk/patient coordinator, and — once revenue justifies it — a practice manager and a device/laser technician.

A 7-figure practice typically runs 1.5–2.5 FTE injectors and 2–3 active treatment rooms.

Patient journey

1. Booking (online CRM / phone)→ 2. Consultation (often required by law as medical evaluation)→ 3. Treatment→ 4. Aftercare instructions→ 5. Follow-up→ 6. Rebooking / membership upsell

A consultation is typically not optional — because injectables and most energy-based devices are "practice of medicine," most state frameworks require a documented medical evaluation before treatment, even if the clinic doesn't charge for it separately.

BPC-157 — the closest thing to a legal pathway opening (with an important correction)

On July 23–24, 2026, the FDA's Pharmacy Compounding Advisory Committee (PCAC) met to consider seven peptides for the 503A Bulks List: BPC-157, KPV, TB-500, and MOTS-c (July 23), plus Emideltide, Epitalon, and Semax (July 24). We downloaded FDA's own briefing document directly (fda.gov/media/193342) rather than relying on secondary summaries, and it changes the picture: FDA staff's own written recommendation to the committee was that NONE of the seven substances — including BPC-157 — be added to the 503A Bulks List. The document states plainly, e.g., "FDA is proposing that BPC-157 (free base) NOT be included on the 503A Bulks List" (and the same language for BPC-157 acetate and all six other substances).

Separately, several industry outlets reported that the advisory committee's actual vote broke from FDA staff's recommendation — an 8–6 vote in favor of recommending BPC-157 for inclusion. We were not able to independently pull the official meeting minutes/voting record confirming that tally from fda.gov within this session, so treat the "8–6" figure as industry-reported, not primary-source-confirmed, while the "FDA staff recommended against inclusion" fact above is a direct quote from FDA's own document. Either way, a PCAC recommendation — for or against — is advisory only. It is not an approval, and it would only open a narrow path for licensed 503A pharmacies to compound BPC-157 under a valid prescription, never retail/OTC sale. BPC-157 remains an unapproved drug today. A further PCAC review of additional peptides is scheduled by end of February 2027.

Method and limits

Read this before quoting any figure

  • Demographics describe the category, not the clinics. Age, gender and interests come from realself.com and botoxcosmetic.com only. Applying them to a single metro practice assumes its customers look like the national category, which is an assumption rather than a measurement.
  • The category weighting is by visits, and RealSelf carries it. At 2.55M against 1.30M, RealSelf holds 66% of the weight. The combined profile leans toward the research audience by construction.
  • Two of the ten metro sites are missing. Montrose Med Spa (Houston) and Rejuvenate Austin were not measured — the free tier's daily lookup limit was reached at the eighth site. Every total and median covers eight.
  • Some metro fields are modelled, not measured. Five of the eight return a bounce rate between 36.7% and 37.2%, and the Manhattan and Charlotte channel mixes match within a point across all ten channels. That tight a cluster across independent sites indicates category-level modelling filling gaps below the measurement threshold.
  • Sheis Beauty (Orlando) is below threshold entirely at 337 visits. Its 100% desktop / 0% mobile split and 1.01 pages per visit are sampling artefacts, not behaviour.
  • Two sites carry heavy non-US traffic. West Palm Beach shows 31.9% from India and Atlanta 17.5%. For single-location clinics that is not a customer base — read those rows US-only.
  • Month-on-month change is one month at a few thousand visits. Read direction, not magnitude.
  • Two panels, two things measured. Similarweb is a web panel — it reports who reached a site, which is shaped by that site's media buying. YouGov is a survey panel reporting stated intent. Where they disagree on age and gender, that disagreement is the finding, not an error in either.
  • The YouGov segment is a proxy. Its taxonomy has no Botox brand and no med spa category; "I would consider plastic surgery" is the nearest intent variable and is broader, since it includes surgery.
  • YouGov region is census-level. "West" and "South" cannot be resolved to a metro, so the regional read is directional against the clinic footprint rather than like-for-like.
  • Everything here is a panel estimate, not measured analytics, and accuracy falls as traffic falls.
